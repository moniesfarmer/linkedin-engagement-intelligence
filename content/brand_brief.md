Six Years in Data — and Still Discovering

I have spent six years working with data — entering it, cleaning it, labeling it, and training AI models on it. For most of that time, I believed I understood what data was. It was not until I completed IBM SkillsBuild's Data Fundamentals and Getting Started with Generative AI certifications in early 2026 that I realized I had only ever been looking at a small corner of something enormous.
Starting in 2019, I built six years of hands-on data experience — from entry-level cleaning to training AI models — while earning certifications from Stanford, Coursera, and ALX.
I thought I had a solid command of the field. Then the IBM courses changed my perspective entirely.
For the first time, I was not just working inside a dataset — I was seeing the full architecture of how data is collected, interpreted, modeled, and deployed. I learned how machine learning models are structured and trained, how generative AI produces language and images, and — most powerfully — how data drives decisions across every major industry on earth.
In finance, data analytics helps banks detect fraud and identify customers at risk of defaulting on loans. In healthcare, patient data powers AI diagnoses and accelerates drug discovery — saving lives in ways that were science fiction a generation ago. In manufacturing, analytics reduces costs by predicting equipment failures and solving supply chain bottlenecks. In retail, companies use buying data to understand customer needs, predict trends, and recommend products with remarkable precision.
Seeing this landscape — the sheer breadth of where data lives and what it can do — has dramatically deepened my curiosity about data utility and access.
My studies also gave me a clearer framework for the skills that define strong data professionals. Beyond technical tools like SQL, Python, Excel, Tableau, and Power BI, the field demands equally important human skills: clear communication, collaborative problem-solving, and — perhaps above all — the ability to tell compelling stories with data. Insights that are never communicated clearly tend to remain hidden, no matter how significant they are.
Three principles have stayed with me from this learning experience: be genuinely curious about the problem and the data; be confident enough to take a position and defend it with evidence; and be adaptable enough to revise that position when the data points elsewhere. Data is a learning process. The best professionals in the field embrace that.
Six years ago, data was something I managed. Today, I understand it as a force that shapes healthcare outcomes, financial systems, manufacturing efficiency, and retail strategy — and one that raises profound questions about ethics, access, and equity that the industry is only beginning to grapple with.
My IBM certifications did not just add lines to my resume. They opened a door I did not know was there. And I intend to keep walking through it.

Why Your Company Doesn't Need to Build a Custom AI Model (And What to Do Instead)

I see this mistake constantly:
Companies think they need to train their own LLM from scratch to get accurate, company-specific answers.

The reality? That's like building your own search engine when Google exists.

The Real Problem:
Standard AI models don't know about YOUR products, YOUR policies, or YOUR data.
Their knowledge stops at their training cutoff date.
The Smart Solution: RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

Here's how it works behind the scenes:

Step 1: Tokenization
Your business documents get broken into tokens (smallest meaningful units).
The AI doesn't read words like humans—it processes numerical representations.
"Q3 sales report" becomes a series of token IDs the model can understand.

Step 2: Create a Knowledge Base
Your documents (policies, manuals, reports) get indexed in a vector database.
Each piece of text becomes searchable numerical embeddings.

Step 3: Intelligent Retrieval
When someone asks "What's our return policy for electronics?"
The system searches your knowledge base and retrieves relevant sections.

Step 4: Context-Aware Response
The retrieved information gets added to the AI's prompt.
Now the AI responds using your actual policies, not generic answers.

Why This Beats Custom Training:
✅ Updates in Minutes, Not Months: Upload new documents → instant access
✅ Fraction of the Cost: No expensive model retraining
✅ Transparency: You can see which documents the AI referenced
✅ Control: Remove outdated info without touching the model

Business Impact:
Customer support teams answer queries using current product specs.
HR departments provide accurate policy information without manual searches.
Sales teams access competitive intelligence from the latest market research.
You don't need to reinvent AI.
You just need to give existing models the right context.

That's the difference between spending millions on custom models vs. spending thousands on smart retrieval.

When I started my journey in data science, I thought Python was just about writing code that worked. But understanding data types—really understanding them—transformed how I solve problems.

Here's what I've learned through practical application:

The Four Pillars
✅ int (Integer) - Whole numbers for counting, indexing, and quantities. Every time I've built loops for annotation tasks or indexed through datasets, integers were the backbone. Operations: +, -, *, //
✅ float (Decimal Numbers) - Measurements and precision. In my AI evaluation work at Invisible Expert Marketplace, accuracy metrics (like my 95% rate) rely on float precision. Operations: +, -, *, /
✅ str (String) - Text, names, dates, labels. Always in quotes. This is where most of my annotation work lives—labeling entities, processing natural language, cleaning text data. The key insight? "25" as a string ≠ 25 as an integer.
✅ bool (Boolean) - True/False logic. Every conditional statement, every filter, every quality check I've implemented uses boolean logic. Operations: and, or, not

Container Types That Scale
✅ list - Ordered and changeable. Perfect for dynamic datasets where I need append() and remove() operations.
✅ tuple - Ordered but fixed. When data integrity matters and you don't want accidental modifications.
✅ dict - Key-value pairs. Game-changer for structured data retrieval. I use this constantly for mapping labels to categories.
✅ set - Unique values only. Brilliant for removing duplicates and finding intersections.

The Real Learning
During my IBM SkillsBuild certification, I learned the syntax. But in six years of data work—from data entry to AI training—I learned that data type determines behavior.
Choosing the wrong type doesn't just cause errors. It impacts performance, storage, and logic flow. That "25" vs 25 distinction? I've debugged that exact issue more times than I'd like to admit.

Practical Takeaway
Before writing any function, I now ask: What type should this be? What operations will I perform? Does mutability matter?

Python's type checking (type() function) became my debugging best friend. When something breaks, 80% of the time it's a type mismatch I didn't catch.

The foundation matters. Master these basics, and everything else becomes logical.

During my recent IBM SkillsBuild certification in Data Fundamentals, I gained valuable insights into the various approaches for labeling unstructured data, a critical skill in annotation work that directly impacts AI model performance.

Unlike structured data that fits neatly into databases, unstructured data (text documents, images, audio files, and videos) requires careful annotation to become machine-readable. The labeling approach you choose can make or break your AI training pipeline.

Key Labeling Methods:

✅ Classification Labeling involves assigning predefined categories to entire data instances. For text, this might mean labeling emails as "spam" or "not spam." For images, it could be tagging photos by object type.

✅ Entity Recognition (NER) identifies and tags specific elements within data. In text annotation, this means highlighting names, locations, dates, or organizations. I've applied this extensively in my work at Invisible Expert Marketplace, where precision in identifying entities directly affects model accuracy.

✅ Bounding Box Annotation is common in computer vision tasks, involving drawing rectangles around objects of interest in images, essential for training object detection models in autonomous vehicles and medical imaging.

✅ Semantic Segmentation goes beyond bounding boxes with pixel-level annotation, assigning labels to every pixel in an image. It's more time-intensive but crucial for applications requiring precise boundaries.

✅ Sentiment and Emotion Labeling assigns emotional states or sentiment polarity to text and audio data. I experienced this firsthand during my work at Babel Audio, where I labeled audio data with specific emotions—a task that powers chatbots, customer service automation, and voice assistants.

✅ Relationship Annotation identifies connections between entities, fundamental for knowledge graphs and advanced NLP applications.

The Annotation Challenge

Quality annotation requires clear guidelines, consistent inter-annotator agreement, and regular quality checks, something I maintain at 95% accuracy in my current role. The IBM course reinforced that annotation isn't just mechanical work; it's a strategic process requiring understanding of both data characteristics and the intended AI use case.

Whether it's atomic action labeling at Atlas Capture, emotion tagging at Babel Audio, or AI evaluation at Invisible Expert Marketplace, the labeling framework you establish sets the stage for model success.

Most consultants jump straight to solutions... but the best ones dig deeper to find the real problem first.

Your client says: "We need a new dashboard because our current reports are confusing."

Most people hear this and immediately start building dashboards.
Smart consultants ask "Why?" five times:

🔍 Why #1: "Why are the current reports confusing?"
 → "The data doesn't match what we see in our daily operations."
🔍 Why #2: "Why doesn't the data match your operations?"
 → "There's a delay between when things happen and when they show up in reports."
🔍 Why #3: "Why is there a delay in the data?"
 → "Our systems don't sync automatically - someone has to manually update everything."
🔍 Why #4: "Why isn't the sync automated?"
 → "We've never had the technical resources to set it up properly."
🔍 Why #5: "Why haven't you allocated technical resources to this?"
 → "We didn't realize how much time our team was wasting on manual updates."

The REAL problem? Not a confusing dashboard, but inefficient processes costing the business hours of manual work daily.

The REAL solution? Process automation, not just better visualization.
The 5 Whys technique helps you: 

✅ Uncover root causes, not just symptoms
✅ Build trust by showing you truly understand their pain
✅ Deliver solutions that actually solve the core issue
✅ Avoid expensive band-aid fixes that don't last

Next time a client presents a problem, resist the urge to solve immediately.
Ask "Why?" five times. You might be surprised what you discover.
